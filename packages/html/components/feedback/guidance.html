Asks “Did this page help you?” at the end of a page, so a service learns which pages fail people, and links to report a problem.

When to use

  • At the end of information pages and the last page of a service, such as a confirmation, so every page of the website reports the same measure.

When not to use

  • In the middle of a form: it takes people away from their task. Ask on the confirmation page instead.
  • When nobody will read the answers. Before you add it, agree who looks at the results each month and what they will change.

How to use

  • Put it after the page's content, before the footer.
  • Send the page's address with the answer (the hidden page field), and count the Yes and No answers for each page. A page with many No answers is the one to research and rewrite first.
  • After an answer, show the same page with the thank-you state, so people stay where they were.
  • Link “Report a problem with this page” to a short form that asks what they were trying to do and what went wrong. Tell people not to type personal details such as their Ghana Card number.
  • Keep only the page, the answer and the date. Do not store who answered, and do not set a cookie for it.

Accessibility

What GGDS does

  • The buttons say what they mean to screen readers: “Yes this page helped me” and “No this page did not help me”.
  • The thank-you message has role="status", so screen readers announce it, and the buttons are at least 44 pixels high.
  • It works without JavaScript: the buttons send an ordinary form.

What you need to do

  • Keep it after the main content, so it is not the first thing people hear.
  • If you send the answer with JavaScript instead, move focus to the thank-you message (it has tabindex="-1").

Research

Alpha: tested automatically, not yet with people on a live website. The question follows the pattern used by government websites that measure every page; check its wording with Ghanaian users and in the GGDS languages.
