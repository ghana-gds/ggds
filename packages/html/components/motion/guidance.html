Parts that rise into view, layers with depth, lines that draw themselves, and scenes that tell a story as people scroll. With no script, and still for anyone who asks for less motion.

When to use

  • On a homepage, a campaign or an information page, to make the page feel alive and to show how things fit together, as the GGDS homepage does.

When not to use

  • Inside a service's steps: questions, check your answers, errors, payment and confirmation. People finishing a task need a still page. A GGDS test checks that no form component or pattern uses these classes.
  • To hide content until people scroll to it, or to make them wait. The words must be there and readable without the movement.
  • On text people read for long. Move pictures, cards and headings, not paragraphs.

How to use

  • Add a class to the part that should move: ggds-reveal on a heading or card, ggds-stagger on a list or grid, ggds-parallax on a picture behind the text, ggds-draw on an SVG drawing (give each path pathLength="1").
  • To tell a story, use a scene: a <section class="ggds-scene ggds-scene--pinned"> holding one ggds-scene__stage with the words and a picture. Give each part of the picture ggds-scene__part and its turn, --2 to --6. The scene stays on screen while people scroll, and its parts arrive in order. Put the words first and the picture after them, as the stage's two children.
  • Choose how a part arrives: it rises by default, or --fade, --grow, --from-start, --from-end or --draw.
  • Keep it small: a few things moving at once, never the whole page.

Accessibility

What GGDS does

  • Nothing moves for people who have asked their device for less motion: they see the finished page (WCAG 2.3.3).
  • Browsers without scroll-driven animations show the same still, finished page. Nothing is ever hidden waiting for an animation.
  • Movement follows the person's own scrolling: nothing plays by itself, and the scroll is never slowed or taken over.
  • Only position, transparency and SVG lines move, so the page never jumps.
  • Cards and list items rise without fading, so their words are at full contrast even when they are only half way on screen. Only pictures and headings fade.
  • A pinned scene stays pinned only on a screen wide and tall enough to hold its words beside its picture. On a phone, at high zoom or on a short screen it is an ordinary section, so nothing is cut off (WCAG 1.4.10). On a phone the picture still builds itself as it rises up the screen, and drifts a little slower than the words, so people never scroll past words that do not move.

What you need to do

  • Write the words so they make sense without the pictures: a scene's heading and text carry its meaning.
  • Give a drawing a text alternative, or hide it from screen readers (aria-hidden="true") when the text already says it all.
